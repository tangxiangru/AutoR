# When claims are counted, saying more is not saying better.

**Routing note.** This is the opposite of the right advice on a benchmark that scores a
report against a checklist, where an uncovered result scores zero and coverage beats
polish. `applies_when` is therefore matched against phrasing specific to the
claim-counted family and not against the word "conclusion", which every brief contains:
an always-on version of this skill would tell a checklist run to say less, and that run
would lose exactly the coverage it is paid for.

A grader that decomposes an answer into atomic claims and asks of each one whether
the reference supports it is scoring a *ratio*. The denominator is how many claims
you made. Every additional sentence is another draw against a reference that was
written to answer one question, and a claim that is true, well-evidenced and
interesting still costs precision if the reference does not happen to mention it.

This inverts the instinct that is correct almost everywhere else, including on the
sibling benchmarks in this repository, where an uncovered result scores zero and
coverage beats polish. Here an uncovered claim costs recall once; a superfluous
claim costs precision permanently.

## The failure this prevents

Three shapes, all of which read as diligence:

- **The trip report.** "We generated N items, ran M models, and observed…" Every
  process sentence is a claim, and the reference makes claims about the world.
- **The hedge.** "Results were mixed", "further work is needed", "this warrants
  investigation". Scored as claims. References almost never make them.
- **The full report pasted in.** Every aside, caveat and method note becomes a
  claim. A long answer can score below a short wrong one.

## The test

Write the answer, then count the independent assertions in it. If a reader could
disagree with a sentence separately from the others, it is a claim. Then, for each:

1. **Is this what the question asked?** Not adjacent, not also-true — asked.
2. **Would the study still be worth reporting if this claim were removed?** If yes,
   remove it.
3. **Is it a claim about the world, or about the work?** "We ran three conditions"
   is about the work. Cut it.

## Numbers, and where they belong

If the grader summarises your text before scoring it, the summariser will delete
concrete values, file names and method names. A conclusion built out of a table is
then scored on whatever generalisation a summariser reads out of it — which may not
be yours.

Run the experiments; they are what makes the direction true, and a claim with
nothing behind it is how a run states the opposite of what it measured. But put the
*direction and the mechanism* in the conclusion — which way it went, and under what
condition it holds or breaks — and leave the numbers in the artifacts, where the
provenance check reads them.

## The shape to aim for

Two to four claims: what the answer to the question is, and the condition that
qualifies it. Prose, no headings, no bullets, no preamble. The register of a
paper's concluding sentence, not of its abstract and not of its results section.
